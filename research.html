Research

Adversity, caregiving, and development across generations

Children’s earliest environments set the stage for how they learn to manage emotions, build relationships, and engage with the world around them. Those environments range from the quality of their relationships with caregivers to the stressors their families face. My research examines how these early experiences shape children’s social-emotional development and broader functioning.

Why do some children exposed to adversity struggle while others adapt well? Which features of their homes and classrooms make the difference? Understanding these risk and resilience processes is a step toward interventions, at home and in school, that target what matters most for children.

These questions also extend across generations. In my prior work, I examined whether and how mothers’ own experiences of childhood adversity carry forward to the next generation, through their caregiving, to shape their children’s development. That work found that mothers’ everyday caregiving, rather than their own adversity histories, predicted their children’s outcomes. My research on dimensions of adversity showed that threat and deprivation shape children’s outcomes through different pathways. Together, these findings pointed me toward the specific processes linking early experience to development.
